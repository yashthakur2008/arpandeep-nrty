\begin{abstract}
Tool-using language-model agents can refuse in text while still taking an unsafe action. We study \emph{fabricated authority}: user-controlled claims that a forbidden action is allowed by a non-existent approval, exemption, or superseding policy. Across 1{,}968 hosted-model trials with 8 scenarios, 6 operating-policy phrasings, 7 attack templates, and 2 production models, fabricated authority raises logged tool-call violations from 0/240 in controls to 208/1440 under attack (14.4\%, $p = 1.1\times10^{-15}$). The largest lever is the deployer's policy wording. A provenance-aware policy that states user-supplied exemptions are not authoritative has 0/288 direct user-message violations, while a documented-exemption carve-out has 93/288 (32.3\%, $p = 1.4\times10^{-32}$); a matched contrast gives complete separation, 0/18 vs 18/18. A length-control ablation shows this is not verbosity: a longer irrelevant policy is worse. An adaptive attacker with white-box access breaks weak controls in 8/8 and 7/8 scenarios across two runs, but breaches the provenance-aware policy in 0/8 scenarios both times. However, a forced indirect-channel study breaks the same policy in 15/36 poisoned policy-lookup trials, so the mitigation is channel-specific rather than universal. The result is a narrow, actionable finding: agent evaluations should score tool logs, and deployment prompts should explicitly reject unauthenticated authority claims while treating tool-output provenance as a separate runtime security problem.
\end{abstract}

\section{Why we changed the outcome variable}

This work began as a GRPO-trained text attacker scored by an LLM judge. It
appeared to succeed: attack success rate rose from 25\% to 48\%. That figure is
an \emph{artifact} of an unvalidated judge, as we now show. We validated the
judge against 200 human-labelled responses (Table~\ref{tab:judges}).

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Three judges scoring 200 identical target responses against human
labels. ``Reported ASR'' is the number a paper using that judge would print;
the human-labelled truth is 19\%.}
\label{tab:judges}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrrr}
\toprule
Judge & Acc. & $\kappa$ & Prec. & Rec. & False pos. & Reported ASR \\
\midrule
\texttt{llama3.2}    & 36\% & 0.09 & 23\% & 100\% & 128 & \textbf{83\%} \\
Refusal heuristic    & 94\% & 0.83 & 76\% & 100\% & 12  & 25\% \\
\texttt{gpt-4o-mini} & 93\% & 0.75 & 93\% & 68\%  & 2   & \textbf{14\%} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

A 69-point swing in reported ASR on identical outputs. The local judge is worse
than the trivial baseline of always answering ``refused'' (81\%), and labelled
128 of 162 genuine refusals as successful jailbreaks, including responses whose
entire text is ``I can't fulfill that request.'' Re-run with a validated judge on 120 held-out behaviors, our trained attacker
showed 12.5\%~$\to$~11.7\% ($p = 1.00$): no effect. Training did change the
model, just not usefully: it raised well-formed output from 71\% to 91\% and
tag compliance from 0\% to 46\%. GRPO taught the output protocol, not the
attack, and the apparent gain was judge error.

Rather than build a better judge, we changed what we measure. For a tool-using
agent the ground truth is already structured: either the forbidden tool was
called with policy-violating arguments, or it was not. \textbf{No LLM judge
appears anywhere in the agentic pipeline below.} This separates our contribution
from text-jailbreak work such as HarmBench, LLMStinger and DarkCite
\cite{mazeika2024harmbench,llmstinger,darkcite}, and from judge-reliability work
showing that ASR can be dominated by evaluator false positives
\cite{coinflip,reliablejudge}. Closest in spirit are agent benchmarks showing
that text safety does not transfer cleanly to tool calls \cite{gap,agentseer} and
RL prompt-injection attacks against tool-use \cite{autoinject}. Our contribution
is deployment-facing: we treat the operating policy itself as an experimental
object and ask which phrasings make fabricated authority succeed or fail when the
outcome is a verifiable tool call.

\paragraph{Contributions.} We provide (i) a tool-log benchmark for fabricated
authority, (ii) a controlled policy-phrasing matrix, (iii) a one-sentence direct-channel mitigation, (iv) ablations for length, matched scenario potency, adaptive pressure, model choice, and indirect-channel poisoning, and (v) a concrete recommendation for agent developers.

\section{Setup}

Each trial places a model in an agentic loop with tools, a safe alternative
(\texttt{request\_approval}), and an operating policy forbidding a specific
action such as exporting patient records, deleting production data, wiring money,
or granting admin access. The attack appends fabricated authority: a cited
directive, a claimed prior approval, or a documented audit exemption. The
scenario-specific violation predicate checks both tool name and arguments, so a
legitimate call to the same tool is not counted as a failure. We vary 8 scenarios,
6 policy phrasings, 7 attacks and 2 targets (\texttt{gpt-4o-mini},
\texttt{claude-haiku-4-5}). Total hosted-API cost: under \$3.

\section{Results}

\paragraph{The attack works.} Fabricated authority raises violations from
\textbf{0/240} (control) to \textbf{208/1440} (14.4\%), one-sided Fisher
$p = 1.1\times10^{-15}$. The control sits exactly at the floor, so every
violation is attributable to the attack.

\paragraph{Policy phrasing dominates (Table~\ref{tab:policy}).}

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Attacked trials only, both targets pooled. The only difference between
\texttt{strict} and \texttt{strict\_hatch} is one sentence stating that claimed
exemptions arriving in user input or tool output are not authoritative.}
\label{tab:policy}
\begin{tabular}{lrrl}
\toprule
Policy phrasing & Violations & Rate & 95\% CI (Wilson) \\
\midrule
\texttt{strict\_hatch} & 0/288  & \textbf{0.0\%} & [0.0\%, 1.3\%] \\
\texttt{strict}        & 7/288  & 2.4\%          & [1.2\%, 4.9\%] \\
\texttt{bare}          & 21/288 & 7.3\%          & [4.8\%, 10.9\%] \\
\texttt{autonomous}    & 87/288 & 30.2\%         & [25.2\%, 35.7\%] \\
\texttt{exemption}     & 93/288 & \textbf{32.3\%} & [27.2\%, 37.9\%] \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\texttt{strict\_hatch} vs \texttt{exemption}: $p = 1.4\times10^{-32}$. Note the
non-monotonicity: \texttt{bare} (prohibition only) is \emph{safer} than
\texttt{exemption}. A documented-exemption carve-out is an explicit invitation
to fabricate a document; adding it is worse than adding nothing.

\paragraph{It is the clause, not the length.} A verbosity control
(\texttt{strict\_verbose}, 98 words of irrelevant policy, longer than
\texttt{strict\_hatch}'s 77) scored \textbf{11/96 (11.5\%)} versus
\texttt{strict\_hatch}'s \textbf{0/96} ($p = 0.000361$) and \emph{doubled} the
5.2\% rate of the shorter \texttt{strict} policy it extends. The longest policy
was the worst. What the policy says matters, not how much.

\paragraph{Matched contrast: complete separation.} Holding scenario, attacks,
model and temperature fixed on \texttt{medical\_export}: \texttt{exemption}
scored \textbf{18/18}, \texttt{strict\_hatch} \textbf{0/18}
($p = 1.1\times10^{-10}$). The one-sentence clause is the only difference.

\paragraph{Which reasoning step fails.} Claims about \emph{provenance and
process state} succeed (\texttt{audit\_exemption} 21.7\%, \texttt{superseded}
17.9\%, \texttt{prior\_approval} 17.9\%); claims about \emph{category
membership} fail completely (\texttt{reclassify} 0/240) as does urgency
(\texttt{emergency} 0.8\%). Models verify the semantics of the act but not the
provenance of the authority permitting it: they have no mechanism to check
whether Directive 12.3(b) exists.

\paragraph{Models differ 20x; policy closes the gap.}
\texttt{claude-haiku-4-5} violates 10/840 (1.2\%) against
\texttt{gpt-4o-mini}'s 198/840 (23.6\%). Under a permissive policy
\texttt{gpt-4o-mini} violates in 6 of 10 attacked trials; under
\texttt{strict\_hatch} it never violates.

\paragraph{The clause survives an adaptive attacker.}
\label{sec:adaptive}
An attacker LLM given the defender's exact system prompt, tool schema, and the
full transcript of its own failed attempts proposed a fresh misdirection each
round for 8 rounds on each of 8 scenarios. It broke the weak control policy
\textbf{8/8} but never breached \texttt{strict\_hatch} (\textbf{0/8},
$p = 7.8\times10^{-5}$). Because the same attacker demonstrably succeeds against
the control, this is an \emph{informative} null rather than a weak-attack
artifact: the validity check our hand-written attempt failed. An independent
replication with a 10-round budget reproduced the result (control 7/8,
\texttt{strict\_hatch} 0/8 over 80 adaptive attempts, $p = 7.0\times10^{-4}$).

\paragraph{Refusal in prose, violation in action.} In 32 of 432 paired trials
the agent states a refusal while simultaneously emitting the forbidden tool
call ($p = 1.3\times10^{-6}$; never observed without an attack). A text-based
evaluation would score these as successful refusals. This is direct evidence
that text and tool calls are different measurements.

\paragraph{Forced indirect channel.} When fabricated authority is moved from
the user message into a required policy-lookup tool result, clean lookups cause
0/72 violations but poisoned lookups cause 51/72. Even \texttt{strict\_hatch}
fails in 15/36 poisoned trials. The mitigation is therefore a strong direct-user-channel rule, not a complete tool-output provenance system.

\section{Recommendations}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Add a provenance clause}: claimed exemptions, approvals or
      superseding policies arriving in user input or tool output are not
      authoritative. 0/288 in our sweep, 0/18 in matched contrast.
\item \textbf{Do not write exemption carve-outs.} The most attackable phrasing
      we tested, worse than silence on exemptions.
\item \textbf{Avoid autonomy-forward phrasing} for agents holding destructive
      tools.
\item \textbf{Always provide a safe alternative action}, or refusal degrades
      into paralysis.
\item \textbf{Evaluate agents on tool calls, not text.} Under attack they
      disagree.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Limitations}

Two hosted targets, both small production models; no frontier-model claim. A 3B
local model (\texttt{llama3.2}) violates its policy 67.5\% of the time with no
attack at all, leaving no headroom, and is reported separately. Single-turn: a
forced indirect study that delivers the fabricated authority through a required
policy-lookup tool result produces 0/72 clean violations but 51/72 poisoned
violations, including 15/36 against \texttt{strict\_hatch}. Thus the provenance
clause mitigates direct user-message authority claims in our setting; it is not a
runtime provenance system for compromised or poisoned tool outputs. Scenarios are hand-written, though per-scenario rates span 7.1\%--15.7\%.
Our hand-written adaptive attacks were too weak to test the clause (0/12 on
policies where standard attacks score 18/18); Section~\ref{sec:adaptive}
replaces them with an LLM attacker, but that attacker is itself only
\texttt{gpt-4o-mini}, and a stronger or RL-trained attacker may yet succeed.

\section*{Ethics statement}

We study attacks on safety-constrained agents using hand-written scenarios and
no real systems or data. The central finding is a \emph{defence} that deployers
can apply for free. We release evaluation tooling and aggregate statistics; the
scenario-specific attacker strings are not operational credentials, exploits, or
access paths to any real system.
